Nearshoring 2026: The Bottleneck Is No Longer Land, It's Power

Mexico's industrial market is normalizing and vacancy remains healthy, but the real constraint on expansion is no longer available land — it's installed electrical capacity.

Mexico's industrial market is entering a maturing phase in 2026: the wave of mass arrivals driven by nearshoring is giving way to consolidation and selective expansion by companies already on the ground. But while the public conversation still centers on square meters and logistics corridors, the latest data from CBRE, Colliers and AMPIP point to a deeper shift: the primary constraint on industrial expansion is no longer land availability — it's installed electrical capacity.

1. The Market Is Normalizing, Not Stalling

  • —Monterrey leads national inventory: per CBRE MarketView (Q1 2026), Monterrey's industrial inventory reached 17.9 million m², a 9.3% annual increase confirming the market's structural expansion.
  • —Solid net absorption, but consolidation-driven: Q1 2026 net absorption totaled 212,000 m², up from 131,000 m² in Q1 2025. However, 87% of that demand came from expansions by companies already installed in the region, not new entrants.
  • —Vacancy still healthy: Monterrey's vacancy stands at 6.9%, up from 5.1% in 2025 — an expected adjustment after the 2022–2024 boom, but still at healthy levels.
  • —Greater Mexico City remains tight: Colliers reports 1.3 million m² under construction in the Valley of Mexico, with vacancy of just 4.2% and active expansion of the Zumpango–AIFA corridor for last-mile logistics.

2. The Real Bottleneck: Power

  • —Widespread supply failures: 91% of companies located in industrial parks report electrical supply failures, and 40% face natural gas shortages.
  • —A growing deficit: a 48,000 MWh deficit is projected over the next five years, against a real 16.7% budget cut at Mexico's federal utility, CFE.
  • —Demand has changed in kind: high compute-density facilities — semiconductors, advanced electronics, AI hardware assembly — require electrical capacity that a traditional industrial park simply cannot provide.

3. Pricing Already Reflects the New Scarcity

  • —Infrastructure investment: AMPIP projects USD $5.83 billion in industrial park investment for 2026, with Nuevo León holding 18% of national industrial park stock, followed by Baja California (16%) and the State of Mexico (12.4%).
  • —Occupancy near capacity in the north: Nuevo León posts 97.2% occupancy across 45+ industrial parks, where demand far outstrips available supply.
  • —Rents trending up: average industrial rent sits around USD $10.32–10.36/m²/month in Mexico City (+11% year over year), USD $7.00–7.07/m²/month in Monterrey, and USD $6.87/m²/month in the Bajío.
  • —Medium-term outlook: PGIM Real Estate forecasts national industrial rent growth near 7% annually between 2025 and 2028, with the Bajío's five-year rent CAGR around 6.3%.

4. Vacancy by Corridor: Where the Pressure Is

  • ·Ciudad Juárez: 9.9% vacancy.
  • ·Monterrey: between 6.9% and 7.9% depending on the quarterly source consulted.
  • ·Querétaro: 6.6% vacancy.
  • ·Chihuahua: 5.5% vacancy.
  • ·Guadalajara: 4.3% vacancy.
  • ·Guanajuato: 3.9% vacancy.
