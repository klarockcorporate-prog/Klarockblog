Data Centers in Mexico: The Project Is Decided Before the Building

Power, water and land are reviewed at conception, not at the end. What the data center panel at the Industrial & Logistics Summit 2026 left on the table, read from the land and the building.

On August 27, 2026, during the Industrial & Logistics Summit organized by Inmobiliare in Mexico City, the panel “La infraestructura digital del nuevo desarrollo industrial” (digital infrastructure for the new industrial development) brought together, among others, representatives of KIO Data Centers and Johnson Controls, moderated by Carlos Ibarra of Ibarra Gallego. The coverage Inmobiliare published on November 3 leaves one underlying idea: a data center is not developed like a conventional industrial building, and its hardest questions are answered before the building exists.

This article is not a recap of the panel. It is a reading from the land and the building: what that order of questions means for anyone who owns a site, a park or a building and is thinking of offering it to a data center operator. What the participants said is attributed to Inmobiliare's coverage; the synthesis and the opinion are Klarock's, and the opinion is marked as such.

1. What was said at the panel

  • —Capital and margin. A data center requires more capital than a traditional industrial building and is a margin business. That is why you need to know the client, the price you will sell to them, the usage intensity they will have and how they will grow before deciding on the infrastructure.Andrés García Fajer, Chief Development Officer at KIO Data Centers
  • —A project built to suit. Every data center is different because its clients and requirements are; it has to be understood as a built-to-suit project (made to the client's specification). The report describes it, in technical terms, as a building that takes in electricity, gives off heat and must be kept at a certain temperature.David Oliver Mader, Real Estate Consultant at Industrial Solutions, according to Inmobiliare's coverage (the technical description is not attributed to a participant)
  • —Power and water from conception. Electrical challenges are reviewed from the start of the project, taking both the water and the energy crises into account. In Querétaro, evaporative systems cannot be used because of the water crisis; using exhaust gases in a cogeneration turbine was mentioned as an alternative.Javier Jiménez Nájera, Regional Account Manager at Johnson Controls
  • —Legal framework. From a legal perspective, five fronts: power supply, water resources, land tenure (to avoid problems with ejido, or communal agrarian, land), tax incentives and data protection.Inmobiliare's coverage (the article does not attribute this to a specific participant)
  • —Querétaro. It became a data center hub “by chance”: one opened, suppliers and carriers arrived, an ecosystem with specialized labor formed, and the city ended up well placed to receive more. García Fajer added that it knew how to welcome them at the right time and grow alongside them.Inmobiliare's coverage; Andrés García Fajer
  • —Equipment and free zones. Practically all specialized equipment comes from the United States, Norway, Italy and China, which creates bottlenecks. Creating free or special zones, with a single window and tax facilities for importing it, was proposed. It was also said that natural gas will be a catalyst; the article does not specify of what.Andrés García Fajer

Jiménez Nájera described Querétaro as a connection point between Mexico City and Texas and said it “is no longer a satellite city of Mexico.”

Summary of Catalina Martinez's coverage in Inmobiliare (November 3, 2026). Klarock did not independently verify the participants' technical claims.

2. What changes compared with a conventional building

Klarock synthesis based on what was said at the panel.

For anyone who knows the industrial building business, the panel marks four differences of method rather than degree:

  • —Capital comes first. More investment per project and a margin business mean decisions have to be made with the client and their consumption on the table, not afterwards.
  • —Design follows the client. Because each project is built to suit, there is no single format that can simply be repeated.
  • —Power and water enter the design, not just the service. They are reviewed from conception because they shape even the cooling system: in Querétaro, for example, they rule out evaporative cooling.
  • —The schedule depends on imported equipment. Bottlenecks in specialized equipment affect when the building can operate.

In August, Klarock analyzed the same tension from the power-grid side: Data Centers in Mexico: When Success Grows Faster than the Power Supply. The panel completes the picture from the project side.

3. The Klarock read

This section is Klarock's opinion, not data from the cited sources.

  1. Physical first, commercial second. If power and water shape the design from conception, they are also the first questions a site must answer. Rent, term and deal structure can usually be negotiated; a lack of power or water cannot.
  2. Clean title is an entry requirement. The legal part of the panel placed land tenure — avoiding problems with ejido land — next to power and water. A site with a tenure dispute can fall out of the conversation even if power is available.
  3. The operator will ask about capacity before square meters. If every project is built to suit and the business lives on margin, the conversation starts with usage intensity, expansion and cooling. Anyone offering land should arrive with those answers in writing.
  4. One scarce resource can put pressure on another. Ruling out evaporative cooling for lack of water pushes the decision toward other solutions, such as more electricity or cogeneration. It is the tension Klarock described in August from the power-grid side.
  5. The schedule is set by the equipment. With imported equipment and bottlenecks, the date of operation depends on the supply chain more than on the civil works. In a sale or lease, timelines and conditions should reflect it.
  6. The single window was a suggestion, not an announcement. García Fajer proposed creating free or special zones for importing equipment; the article does not mention an initiative under way. No site should be valued on the assumption that they will exist.

4. What to check before talking to an operator

  • —Power: feasible supply capacity, interconnection timelines and backup.
  • —Water: local availability and restrictions, and whether evaporative cooling is viable in the area (in Querétaro, according to the panel, it is not).
  • —Land tenure: clean title; if the site is or was ejido land, its regime and pending procedures reviewed with legal advice.
  • —Tax incentives and data protection: part of the legal framework the panel mentioned; worth identifying with legal advice from the start.
  • —Ecosystem: suppliers, carriers and specialized labor near the site.
  • —Scale and expansion: the ability to grow in phases, depending on the client's usage intensity.
  • —Equipment schedule: import lead times for critical equipment and their effect on the date of operation.

What it means for each player

  • —Landowners and building owners: put together the capacity file — power, water and title — before looking for an operator. A documented site can be evaluated with more certainty than one that only offers area and location.
  • —Developers: the product is a build-to-suit project. The opportunity is not in having a standard building ready, but in arriving with power, water and land resolved.
  • —Investors: in a high-capital, margin-driven business, it pays to know before building the variables the panel flagged as conditions: power, water, land tenure and equipment lead times.
