The Next Industrial Frontier in the Valley of Mexico: The Market Moved Before the Map

When the main corridor fills up and gets expensive, demand does not vanish: it moves. The route it takes shows where jobs, retail and services will concentrate next.

In August 2026 the official delimitation of the Valley of Mexico Metropolitan Area (ZMVM) went from 76 to 84 municipalities and boroughs: seven more municipalities from Hidalgo and one from Morelos, Huitzilac. The declaration was published in the Official Gazette (DOF) on August 25. For the industrial market the news came late: developers and companies had spent years looking for land, connectivity, power and costs outside the most consolidated corridors and, according to Spot2.mx, industrial capital was already operating that region as part of the ZMVM before the map recognized it.

This article offers one reading: when the main corridor fills up and becomes expensive, demand does not disappear; it moves, and the route it takes anticipates where jobs, retail and services will concentrate next. It also warns that the new frontier is not a single corridor but a network of nodes with different specialties, and that power is starting to weigh as much as land.

1. The corridor that ran out of room

For years, the Cuautitlán–Tultitlán–Tepotzotlán (CTT) corridor was the country's logistics node of choice: it sits on the Mexico City–Querétaro highway and a few kilometers from Mexico's largest consumer market. That advantage is now also its limit.

With little supply and rising rents, expansion looks for other routes. According to Spot2.mx, when Class A inventory becomes scarcer in the CTT, expansion starts to shift toward emerging corridors such as Huehuetoca, the AIFA–Zumpango axis (around the Felipe Ángeles International Airport) and Tepeji del Río.

A note on method: the figures come from different firms, with definitions of inventory, absorption and availability that do not match. They show direction and scale; they should not be added up or compared point by point.

On rents: the gap between the new corridor and the traditional one is smaller than often assumed. According to Publimetro (October 14, 2026), using Spot2.mx data, the average rent in AIFA–Zumpango is ~USD 10.12 per m² per month, close to ~USD 10.40 in Cuautitlán. Rent alone does not explain the shift.

3. Not one corridor: a network of nodes

Zumpango looks like the logistics phenomenon we already saw in the CTT: large buildings, fast absorption and proximity to AIFA. Spot2.mx also identifies Huehuetoca and Tepeji del Río as emerging corridors. Tula is something else: a region with a long-standing manufacturing and energy vocation.

For anyone deciding where to locate, the consequence is practical: each node calls for different questions. In Zumpango, watch the pressure on land and services; along the Tula–Atotonilco–Atitalaquia axis, growth coexists with environmental and water-infrastructure challenges. According to Excélsior, Semarnat announced in 2026 an investment of around 900 million pesos to clean up the Tula and Salado rivers, with collector lines in the Tula, Atotonilco and Atitalaquia area.

4. Power now competes with land

Available square meters are no longer the only variable. For advanced manufacturing or data centers, the underlying question is whether there will be enough electrical capacity from day one of operation. Vianey Macías, Head of Market Research at Spot2.mx, argues that the decisive factor for investing is no longer the availability of square meters but of megawatts; for that reason Spot2.mx identifies Toluca–Lerma as the next piece of the map. It is the same pattern Klarock has documented in other markets: the limit is not always the land, but the infrastructure that makes it operable.

5. The Klarock read

This section is Klarock's opinion, not data from the cited sources.

  1. Industrial instinct is a leading indicator, not a forecast. A building's location does not guarantee a city, but when land, highways, power and labor line up, economic activity tends to concentrate.
  2. Pressure on the CTT will keep pushing demand outward as long as inventory stays full and little land remains for large-format projects. With rents currently close between Zumpango and Cuautitlán, the driver seems to be the availability of land and buildings rather than price.
  3. The effect does not stop at the building. Where jobs concentrate, neighborhood retail, services and transport follow. For commercial real estate, the surroundings of the new industrial nodes are a signal to watch from now on.
  4. Electrical capacity will be an entry filter. An attractive site without certainty of power can fall out of the conversation with the highest-paying tenants.

6. What to check before locating in an emerging node

  • —Power: contracted or feasible supply capacity, connection timelines and backup.
  • —Water and water infrastructure: availability, quality and pressure on municipal services.
  • —Real connectivity: travel times to consumer markets, ports and customs, not just distance in kilometers.
  • —Labor: local availability and commute times.
  • —Land tenure and zoning: the legal and land-use status of the site.
  • —Future supply: square meters under construction, not just existing inventory.
  • —The cost of waiting: compare the emerging node's rent with the consolidated corridor's and with the cost of moving the operation.

What it means for each player

  • —Landowners: value may be running ahead of urbanization. It pays to know which nodes have power and connectivity, and which only have a low price.
  • —Developers: the opportunity is not just arriving first, but arriving with power, water and surroundings resolved.
  • —Industrial tenants: moving to an emerging node can lower rent but changes the operation; evaluate total cost, not just the price per m².
